Module 5 Theory Supplement: PCA vs SVD

This section requires some background in linear algebra.

PCA vs Singular Value Decomposition

PCA is connected to the Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) of the data matrix \(X\). To be precise, suppose that we have a set of centered predictor vectors \(X_1, ...X_p\) all of length \(n\) (if they are not centered, just subtract the column wise means). Define the data matrix \(X = \begin{bmatrix} X_1 & X_2 & \cdots & X_p\end{bmatrix}\). The SVD of \(X\) is \[ X = U \Sigma V^T \]

where \(U,V\) are orthogonal matrices whose columns are called the left and right singular vectors of \(X\), respectively, and \(\Sigma\) is a diagonal matrix whose diagonal entries are called the singular values. Note that the singular values are the square roots of the eigenvalues of the symmetric matrix \(X^TX\) since \[ X^TX = (V \Sigma^T U^T)(U\Sigma V^T) = V \Sigma^T \Sigma V^T \] The matrix \(V\) is the corresponding matrix of eigenvectors. To get \(U\), we take the eigenvectors of \(XX^T\) instead.

The SVD can be rewritten as \[ XV = U \Sigma \]

which has \(j\)th column

\[ Xv_j = \sigma_j u_j \]

The \(u_j\) (scaled by \(\sigma_j\)) are the Principal Component scores (orthogonal linear combinations of the columns of \(X\)) and the \(v_j\) are the corresponding loadings. Therefore, \(V\) is the rotation matrix and the PC scores are \(U \Sigma\).

Now to understand the “proportion of variance part” requires going a little deeper. If our \(X\) is \(n\times p\) (\(n\) data points, \(p\) predictors), then its sample covariance matrix is1 \[ C= \frac{1}{n-1} X^TX \]

The total variance in the \(X_i\) would be the trace of this matrix: \(tr(C) = \sum C_{ii}\). But also notice that \[ C = \frac{1}{n-1} X^T X = V \left(\frac{1}{n-1}\Sigma^T\Sigma\right) V^T \]

or

\[ CV= V \Lambda \]

with \(\Lambda = \Sigma^T\Sigma/(n-1)\). Behold, the eigenvalue-eigenvector equation emerges and the eigenvalues of \(C\) are \(\lambda_i = \sigma_i^2/(n-1)\): scaled versions of the singular values of \(X\)!

Furthermore, since \(V\) is orthogonal, we have \[ tr(C) = tr(V\Lambda V^T) = tr(\Lambda) \] so the total variance is also \[ \sum \lambda_i \]

and the proportion coming from the \(j\)th term is \[ \frac{\lambda_j}{\sum \lambda_i }= \frac{\sigma_j^2}{\sum \sigma_i^2} \]

To summarize:

  • The principal component scores are the left singular vectors of \(X\) multiplied by the corresponding singular values.
  • The loadings are the right singular vectors of \(X\) (or equivalently, the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix of the centered data)
  • The variance of the PCs are the squared singular values of \(X\) divided by \(n-1\) (or equivalently, the eigenvalues of the covariance matrix)
  • The total variance is the sum of squared singular values divided by \(n-1\) (or equivalently the sum of the eigenvalues of the covariance matrix)

Connection to Biplots.

The SVD \(X=U\Sigma V^T\) gives us options on how to represent the row and column coordinates in the rotation matrix. In the printout from prcomp(), the rotation matrix is \(V\) and the transformed PCs are \(U \Sigma\).

In a distance biplot (scale = 0), this is essentially what is displayed. The observations are represented by \(U \Sigma\) and the variables by \(V\). This preserves distances between the observations as well as possible in the lower-dimensional projection (the data have merely been rotated by \(V\) into PCs). The first two columns of \(U \Sigma\) give the first two PCs and these are what fviz_pca_ind plots.

But if we instead choose to keep \(\Sigma\) with \(V\), we can represent the variables as \(V \Sigma\) and the observations using \(U\) instead. That is basically what we see in a correlation biplot (scale = 1). The covariance between the \(i\)th variable \(X_i\) and the \(j\)th principle component \(PC_j = (XV)_j\) is the \(ij\)th component of the matrix \[ cov(X,XV) = \frac{1}{n-1} X^TXV = \frac{1}{n-1} V \Sigma^T \Sigma \]

Assuming the \(X_i\) are scaled to have variance 1, this means the correlation between \(X_i\) and \((XV)_j\) is \[ \frac{\sigma_i^2 v_{ij}/(n-1)}{\sigma_i/\sqrt{n-1}} = \sigma_i v_{ij}/\sqrt{n-1} \] which explains why keeping the \(\sigma\)’s with the \(V\) gives rise to the “correlation” biplot (or what is seen when running fviz_pca_var()).

To look at this another way, going back to the SVD \[ X = U \Sigma V^T \] we could break apart \(\Sigma = \Sigma^\alpha \Sigma^{1-\alpha}\) for any \(0 \leq \alpha \leq 1\). (The power makes sense since \(\Sigma\) is diagonal with nonnegative entries.) We can then choose to plot points as \(U \Sigma^{1-\alpha}\) and variables as \(\Sigma^{\alpha} V^T\). Taking \(\alpha = 0\) gives distance, \(\alpha = 1\) gives correlation, and \(\alpha = 1/2\) leads to the balanced approach of fviz_pca_biplot().
